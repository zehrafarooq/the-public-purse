What the Labourer's
Day Buys.

An explanatory brief on Pakistan's wage purchasing power, week ending 23 April 2026 — what a day of construction labour bought across seventeen cities, where it bought less, and what the geography of that variation reveals about the structure of the economy.

By Zehra Farooq Reference week · 23 April 2026 Reading time · ~14 minutes
Scroll to begin
I The numbers that describe the system

What Pakistan weighs,
and what it pays for it.

Sensitive Price Indicator. Essential commodity prices. Daily wage rates. City coverage. Five terms that frame every conversation about Pakistani household cost-of-living — defined, then populated with the figures from the PBS bulletin for the week ending 23 April 2026.

Pakistan's high-frequency price data is published in a single weekly bulletin. The Sensitive Price Indicator — usually called by its abbreviation, SPI — is released every Friday by the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics. It tracks the price of fifty-one essential commodities across seventeen cities, reporting minimum, average, and maximum prices observed during the week. It also tracks daily wage rates for five construction trades across the same seventeen cities. The bulletin runs to four pages. It is the most granular, most regular cost-of-living dataset Pakistan publishes.

The price of a 20-kilogram bag of wheat flour in Lahore on 23 April 2026 was Rs 1,810. The same bag in Sukkur was Rs 2,040. A construction labourer in Lahore that week earned Rs 1,747 a day. A construction labourer in Sukkur earned Rs 900. The two ratios — of prices, and of wages — do not move together. Their interaction across cities and across items is the subject of this brief.

The five terms, defined

Sensitive Price Indicator (SPI) is the weekly bulletin in which the figures used here are reported. PBS publishes it under the authority of the Federal Bureau of Statistics Act, on the Friday following the close of the reference week.

Essential commodity is any of the fifty-one items in the SPI basket — staples, kitchen essentials, energy items, and a small number of durables. The basket includes wheat flour, cooking oil, vegetable ghee, milk, mutton, beef, pulses, vegetables, eggs, sugar, tea, salt, soap, matches, electricity (per unit), gas (per MMBTU), petrol, diesel, and LPG cylinders.

City average price is the mean of minimum, average, and maximum observations recorded in each city during the reference week. PBS surveys outlets across each city and reports the city-level average in Appendix-A of the bulletin.

Daily wage rate is the prevailing rate paid to a worker in the relevant trade for one day's work in the reference city, surveyed and reported in Appendix-B Section D of the same bulletin. The five trades surveyed are painter, mason, labourer, plumber, and electrician. The wage figures are observational, not collectively bargained.

Days of work, as used in this brief, is the ratio of an item's price to a labourer's daily wage. It expresses, in days of construction labour, the cost of acquiring one unit of the item. A figure of 1.04 days means the worker must labour for one day and one hour to afford the item; a figure of 2.27 days means two days and roughly two and a half hours.

Two observations follow directly from the table. First, the variation in labourer wages across cities, at 1.94×, exceeds the variation in wheat flour prices across cities, at 1.30×. The same item moves through space at substantially smaller multiples than the wage of the worker who buys it. Second, the cities reporting the lowest labourer wages — Sukkur and Larkana, both Rs 900 per day — do not report the lowest item prices. The cities at the bottom of the wage ladder do not enjoy a corresponding price discount.

The arithmetic of these two observations is the starting point of everything that follows: in a country where prices vary across cities by ratios under 1.5×, and wages vary across cities by ratios approaching 2×, the geography of purchasing power is governed primarily by the wage side. The next chapter takes the calculation a step further, and applies it to a basket.

II How the household basket is structured

Eight items,
one working week.

Out of the fifty-one items the SPI reports, eight describe most of a working household's weekly outlay. Sized into realistic single-purchase quantities, they form a basket whose total cost is in the same order of magnitude as a labourer's weekly take-home.

The SPI basket of fifty-one items is not designed for direct comparison against wages. Items are reported in their natural retail units — one kilogram of beef, one dozen eggs, one litre of petrol — which are not the units in which a household buys them. To convert the bulletin's per-unit prices into something a working family would recognise, this brief sizes each item to a typical single-purchase quantity.

Eight items are selected. They are not the only items a household buys; they are the items that, together, capture the majority of weekly outlay for a Pakistani working household, on the evidence of household expenditure surveys.

The basket and its sizing

The eight items, with their sizing rationale, are: a 20-kilogram bag of wheat flour, equivalent to roughly one month's atta for a family of five; a 5-litre tin of cooking oil, the standard retail size; one kilogram of mutton, a single occasion's meat; ten litres of motor petrol, equivalent to one motorbike fill or part of a small-vehicle fill; five litres of fresh milk, equivalent to a working family's weekly consumption; two dozen eggs, equivalent to a working family's weekly consumption; one kilogram of vegetable ghee, equivalent to roughly one week's cooking-fat use; and one kilogram of dry lentils, the standard retail purchase.

For each item, the city-level average price reported in Appendix-A of the SPI is multiplied by the quantity multiplier above. The product is the weekly basket cost in that city. Dividing that cost by the city's labourer daily wage gives the days of work required to acquire it.

The basket is deliberately sparse. A more comprehensive basket would add rent, fuel for cooking, electricity bill, school fees, transport fares, and clothing. Some of these are reported in the SPI (electricity per unit, LPG per cylinder, gas per MMBTU); others are not. The eight items chosen here are limited to those for which the SPI provides direct, city-level price observations on the reference date. Any household basket constructed from these items will be conservative: it understates the total purchase burden by excluding rent, services, and durables.

The eight items chosen here capture most of a working household's weekly outlay. None of them captures the rent, the bills, or the school fees.

III The two-city case

Lahore and Sukkur,
same basket.

Pakistan's seventeen surveyed cities range across nearly the full spread of wages and prices. Two — Lahore at the top of the wage distribution, Sukkur near the bottom — frame the argument. Their baskets cost almost the same. Their workers earn almost twice as differently.

Of the seventeen cities surveyed, Lahore reports the highest construction labourer daily wage at Rs 1,747. Sukkur, in upper Sindh, reports one of the lowest at Rs 900. The wage gap between them, expressed as a ratio, is 1.94×. The gap expressed in level terms is Rs 847 per day, or about USD 3 per day.

Two observations follow from the table.

First, in rupee terms the two baskets differ by less than five per cent. The Lahore basket totals Rs 13,564; the Sukkur basket totals Rs 13,002. The Sukkur basket is in fact slightly cheaper — by Rs 562, or about four per cent — driven by lower mutton, slightly cheaper wheat flour relative to Lahore, and modestly cheaper produce. On the price side, the two cities are almost equivalent.

Second, in days-of-work terms the two baskets diverge sharply. The Lahore labourer must work 7.77 days to acquire the basket. The Sukkur labourer must work 14.45 days. The ratio between them is 1.86× — almost identical to the wage ratio of 1.94×. The basket-cost ratio is 0.96×. The conclusion is arithmetic. The work-burden gap is, very nearly, the wage gap. Prices contribute almost nothing to it.

The same calculation, run for any pair of cities in the SPI panel, returns broadly the same finding. The work-burden of an essential basket varies across Pakistan primarily because wages vary, not because prices do.

The pattern in the table is geographic rather than statistical. The spread between the city at the top of the ranking and the city at the bottom is 1.93×. A Larkana labourer works 93 per cent more days for the same basket than a Lahore labourer. Within Sindh, the spread between Karachi and Larkana is 1.55×; within Punjab, between Lahore and Bahawalpur, it is 1.84×. The variation is not principally between provinces but within them.

The mechanism behind the ranking

Three structural features explain the pattern. First, labour markets in Pakistan are deeply local. Construction wages reflect the local supply of unskilled labour relative to local demand for it; in cities with large agricultural hinterlands and limited industrial employment alternatives, construction wages settle low. Second, item prices, by contrast, are set by national wholesale chains for processed goods (cooking oil, ghee, eggs, milk powder), by import-parity formulas for fuel, and by provincial procurement and federal support for wheat. None of these national mechanisms generates city-level price discounts that match local wage gaps. Third, agricultural commodities — wheat, vegetables, milk — show some city-level price variation reflecting local supply, but the variation is modest relative to wage variation.

The result is the table above: prices vary across cities at single-digit-percentage scales; wages vary at scales approaching two-fold. The resulting work-burden geography reflects the wage axis.

V What sets the price

Three categories,
three burdens.

The same administered, captured, and competitive grouping that organises Pakistan's price-trajectory data — examined in The Public Purse · No. 02 — applies cleanly to the level data of a single SPI week. National medians, computed by group, separate by a factor of nine.

The brief preceding this one — The Three Inflations — divided Pakistan's CPI sub-indices into three groups by who effectively sets the price. Administered prices are notified by regulators and ministries. Captured prices are set by private-sector actors operating in markets with structurally weak competition. Competitive prices emerge from markets with many sellers, low entry barriers, and import or export discipline. The grouping is the author's, and the boundaries are debatable. Applied to the level data of a single SPI week, rather than the trajectory data of a decade, the grouping produces a similarly ordered separation.

For each of fifteen items in the SPI basket — five administered, six captured, four competitive — the national-average city price is divided by the national-average labourer wage to give a national days-of-work figure. The medians by group return the figures below.

The medians of the three groups order themselves clearly. The administered group, at 2.79 days per typical purchase, is the heaviest. The captured group, at 1.08 days, is roughly two and a half times lighter. The competitive group, at 0.31 days, is approximately nine times lighter than the administered group. The ratio between the heaviest and lightest groups is 9.0×.

Two observations follow. First, the level data corroborate the trajectory data from No. 02 — administered prices and captured prices both impose larger burdens than competitive prices. Second, on this single-week snapshot the administered median exceeds the captured median, the reverse of the trajectory finding. The reasons are mechanical. The administered category here includes LPG and motor fuel, which are import-parity items priced at world levels; over a decade they cumulated less than the captured edible-oils group, but at any given level they remain among the heaviest single purchases a household makes. The captured category, conversely, includes items like fresh milk and powdered milk that have small per-unit prices despite high cumulative growth.

The two findings are consistent. The trajectory shows where prices have run furthest. The level shows where prices currently stand. They answer different questions.

Where there is competition, a labourer's day buys nine times more than where the state administers and the cartels capture.

VI What the measurement misses

Three frames
outside the bulletin.

The SPI is methodologically careful within its scope. Three specific design choices, however, mean the figures presented in this brief understate the work-burden of essential household consumption for distinct subsets of the workforce.

This chapter does not argue that the SPI is wrong. It is constructed for a specific purpose — high-frequency tracking of essential prices — and it does that consistently. It does describe three design choices that cause specific elements of household experience to fall outside the measurement.

Snapshot, not trajectory

The figures in this brief are drawn from a single SPI bulletin, the week ending 23 April 2026. Each weekly bulletin is an instantaneous reading. A days-of-work calculation for any other week would return different values; the wage and price levels both move week to week. The findings reported here describe the structure of Pakistan's wage-price geography on one specific date. They do not describe whether the structure is widening, narrowing, or stable across time. Constructing a multi-year trajectory would require parsing the historical SPI bulletin archive, which is published weekly and accessible from the PBS website but is not included in the analysis here.

The construction labourer is a partial index

The wages used in this brief are construction labourer wages — the rate paid to an unskilled worker on a building site, surveyed in seventeen cities. This is a real and economically important wage. It is not a comprehensive index of Pakistani labour income. It excludes agricultural day-labour, which dominates rural Sindh and southern Punjab and is typically paid at lower rates than urban construction work. It excludes domestic work, which is heavily female and largely informal. It excludes the salaried lower-middle class — clerks, teachers in private schools, administrative staff — whose monthly earnings, divided by twenty-six working days, yield daily figures that differ from construction wages in either direction depending on city and sector. Households whose primary earner is in any of these categories face days-of-work figures that differ from those reported here.

Single-week prices, multi-week budgets

Households do not buy the basket in a single transaction. Wheat flour is purchased monthly, mutton occasionally, eggs and milk weekly, vegetable ghee weekly, petrol on a rolling basis, lentils as needed. The basket constructed in this brief expresses what one week's worth of the canonical basket would cost; whether a working family actually consumes the basket every week depends on income, savings, and competing claims. The days-of-work figures reported here are arithmetic ratios, not behavioural claims. They describe how many days of construction labour would be required, at the reference week's prices and wages, to acquire the items listed. They do not describe how households actually allocate their time and earnings.

Pakistan's Sensitive Price Indicator, on the bulletin for the week ending 23 April 2026, reports the prices of fifty-one essential commodities and the daily wages of five construction trades across seventeen cities. The bulletin is a single weekly snapshot, published every Friday, and available continuously from the Pakistan Bureau of Statistics.

An eight-item household basket priced for that reference week, divided by the local construction labourer wage, returns days-of-work figures ranging from 7.77 in Lahore to 14.96 in Larkana — a ratio of 1.93×. The Lahore and Sukkur baskets, taken on their own, cost almost the same in rupee terms; the Sukkur labourer works 1.86× as many days for the basket because the wage gap between the two cities is 1.94×.

Sorted by who sets the price, the items in the basket arrange themselves into three persistent burden levels. Administered purchases — LPG cylinders, fuel tanks, electricity bills, atta bags — register a national median of 2.79 days. Captured-market purchases — cooking oil, ghee, mutton, beef, milk — register 1.08 days. Competitive purchases — eggs, lentils, vegetables, fruit — register 0.31 days. The ratio between the heaviest and lightest groups is approximately 9×.

Within Pakistan, the days-of-work geography reflects the wage axis more than the price axis. Item prices vary across the seventeen surveyed cities by single-digit-percentage multiples. Construction labourer wages vary by ratios approaching two-fold. The wage gap is what produces the work-burden gap. What individual readers conclude from the description is their own work.

Glossary & sources.

Key terms used in this brief

Sensitive Price Indicator (SPI)
The weekly PBS bulletin tracking prices of essential commodities and daily wages of selected trades across seventeen cities. Released every Friday following the close of the reference week.
Essential commodity
Any of the fifty-one items in the SPI basket — staples, kitchen essentials, energy items, and selected durables.
City average price
The mean of minimum, average, and maximum observations recorded in each city during the reference week, as reported in Appendix-A of the SPI bulletin.
Daily wage rate
The prevailing rate paid to a worker in the relevant trade for one day's work in the reference city, surveyed and reported in Appendix-B Section D of the SPI bulletin.
Days of work
The ratio of an item's price to a labourer's daily wage. The number of days of construction labour required to acquire one unit of the item.
Basket total
The sum of city-level prices for an eight-item household basket, sized to typical single-purchase quantities. Reported in rupees and in days of work.
Quantity multiplier
The factor by which a per-unit SPI price is multiplied to give a typical household single-purchase quantity (e.g., petrol × 10 for a 10-litre tankful, milk × 5 for a five-litre weekly purchase).
Basket cost ratio
The ratio of basket totals between two cities, measured in rupees. Across the seventeen-city panel for the reference week, basket cost ratios fall within a narrow range.
Wage ratio
The ratio of construction labourer daily wages between two cities. Across the seventeen-city panel for the reference week, wage ratios approach 2×.
Work-burden ratio
The ratio of days-of-work figures between two cities, equal to the basket cost ratio divided by the wage ratio. In the data presented here, work-burden ratios are dominated by wage ratios.
Administered prices
Prices set by regulators or ministries — petrol and diesel (OGRA), LPG (OGRA-determined producer price plus marketing margin), electricity tariff (NEPRA), wheat-flour support and procurement (provincial governments).
Captured prices
Prices set in private-sector markets with structurally weak competition — oligopoly, professional self-regulation, or informal market structures. Includes cooking oil, vegetable ghee, mutton, beef, fresh milk, and powdered milk in this brief.
Competitive prices
Prices emerging from markets with many sellers, low entry barriers, and import or export discipline. Includes eggs, lentils, fruit, vegetables, and long cloth in this brief.
OGRA
Oil and Gas Regulatory Authority. Federal regulator for petroleum products, gas tariffs, and LPG.
NEPRA
National Electric Power Regulatory Authority. Federal regulator for electricity tariffs and Fuel Cost Adjustments.
Construction labourer
The unskilled-labour wage category surveyed in Appendix-B Section D of the SPI. Other surveyed trades — painter, mason, plumber, electrician — typically command higher rates than the labourer figure used in this brief.
